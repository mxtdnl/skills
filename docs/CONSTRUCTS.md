## How to read this document

Each skill has:

- a **working definition** in one sentence;
- two to four **facets**: the parts of the skill the tool measures and develops separately;
- for each facet, a definition and **observable behaviours**: at least two of an undergraduate who is strong, and at least two of one who is still developing;
- **out of scope**: what the skill does *not* include in this tool, so that skills do not overlap and scores are not over-read;
- **planned measures**, from `audit/AUDIT-REPORT.md` section 5, mapped to facets in a coverage table;
- **decisions and open questions**: the owner's answers so far, and anything still to be settled by Hult.

"Developing" describes a point on a path, not a deficit. The behaviours are examples to guide item writing and coaching; they are not a checklist or a scoring rubric.

Facet codes (for example `CO1`) are used in the item bank and in data exports. Once a skill is approved, its codes do not change meaning; a facet that is redefined gets a new code.

Citations are given only where a facet draws on published work that the author is confident exists. Bibliographic details were written from memory and should be checked before this document is circulated outside the project.

## 1. Collaboration

**Working definition.** Working with others towards a shared goal by sharing and seeking information, coordinating effort, and handling disagreement in a way that keeps the work moving.

### CO1. Information sharing and integration

Making sure the team has what it needs to decide well: offering what you know, asking for what others know, and combining the two. This includes making sure that every member is heard; inclusion is part of this facet, not a separate one. Informed by research on hidden profiles, which shows that groups tend to discuss information everyone already holds and neglect information only one member holds (Stasser & Titus, 1985).

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| Shares information that others are unlikely to have, without being asked | Shares mainly what everyone already knows |
| Asks quieter members directly for their view or knowledge before a decision | Accepts the first proposal before everyone has contributed |

### CO2. Coordination and contribution

Organising the work so that it gets done: agreeing who does what, following through, and keeping the team on track.

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| Proposes a clear division of tasks and deadlines, and checks progress against them | Waits for others to organise the work |
| Delivers their own share on time, or warns the team early when they cannot | Misses deadlines without warning, or quietly redoes other people's work instead of raising the problem |

### CO3. Constructive disagreement and feedback

Raising problems and differences of view in a way that improves the team's work and keeps the team working together.

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| Raises a concern about the team's direction early, gives a reason, and supports the decision once it is made | Stays silent about concerns, or keeps reopening decisions after they are made |
| Gives a teammate specific, respectful feedback about a problem with their work, directly to that person | Complains about the teammate to others, or ignores the problem |

### Out of scope

- Leadership as a formal role.
- Friendliness, popularity or general personality (for example, agreeableness).
- The quality of the team's final product.
- Generosity or cooperation in economic games. The legacy Public Goods Game is removed (audit A-5).

A solo online task can measure collaboration-relevant *judgement* and behaviour with *disclosed* simulated teammates. It cannot fully evidence real collaborative behaviour, which is best observed in real group work, for example through structured peer evaluation (Ohland et al., 2012). Peer evaluation is planned for a later integration with Hult systems (audit section 3, point 3). Results must say this.

### Planned measures

| Code | Measure | Type |
|---|---|---|
| CO-SR | Self-report, balanced keying | Self-report |
| CO-T1 | Situational judgement test: team scenarios, response options rated for effectiveness; keys set by a Hult expert panel | Judgement |
| CO-T2 | Hidden-profile task with disclosed simulated teammates: success requires sharing and asking for unshared information | Behaviour (simulated) |
| CO-T3 | Structured peer evaluation from real group work (later, Hult-systems integration) | Peer rating |

### Coverage

| Facet | CO-SR | CO-T1 SJT | CO-T2 Hidden profile | CO-T3 Peer (later) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CO1 Information sharing and integration | ✓ | ✓ (some scenarios) | ✓ primary | ✓ |
| CO2 Coordination and contribution | ✓ | ✓ | | ✓ |
| CO3 Constructive disagreement and feedback | ✓ | ✓ | | ✓ |

**Gap.** Until peer evaluation exists, CO2 and CO3 rest on self-report and judgement only; neither observes behaviour.

### Decisions and open questions

**Decided by the owner (26 November 2026):**

- Inclusion stays within CO1 (see the CO1 definition).
- SJT scenarios use a mix of Hult's own group-work settings (for example, multicultural project teams) and generic settings. Scenarios set at Hult must still pass the cultural-neutrality checks in `docs/REVIEW-CHECKLIST.md`.

**Still open:**

1. **Keying the situational judgement test.** An SJT has no answer that is correct by logic; its key is the judgement of experienced people about how effective each response option is. Hult needs to decide:
   - *who* the panel is: for example, faculty who teach or assess group work, and coaches;
   - *how many* panel members rate each option independently;
   - *how the key is set*: for example, the mean of the panel's effectiveness ratings for each option;
   - *when an item is dropped*: for example, when panel members disagree strongly about which option is most effective.

   No numbers are proposed here: the panel size and the agreement rule should be fixed by Hult and recorded in `docs/SCORING.md` before the SJT is scored (S10).

## 2. Curiosity

**Working definition.** The drive to notice gaps in one's own knowledge and to seek out new information, questions and perspectives, beyond what a task requires.

This definition follows the information-gap account of curiosity (Loewenstein, 1994): curiosity arises when a person notices that they know some, but not all, of something. Measuring it through choices that carry a cost, relative to what the person already knows, follows Kang et al. (2009).

### CU1. Information seeking

Choosing to find out more, especially where one recognises a gap in one's own knowledge, even when it costs time or effort.

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| Spends limited time or effort on finding out things they know they do not know | Seeks information only when required, or only on topics already familiar |
| Follows up a puzzling fact by looking for the explanation | Accepts a surprising fact without wanting to know why |

### CU2. Questioning

Asking questions that probe causes, explanations and implications rather than only surface facts.

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| Asks "why", "how" and "what if" questions about a new situation | Asks mainly "what", "when" and "who" questions, or few questions at all |
| Asks questions whose answers would change how the situation is understood | Asks questions that only confirm details already given |

### CU3. Openness to the unfamiliar

Engaging willingly with unfamiliar subjects, methods and viewpoints, including ones that challenge one's own.

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| Chooses to explore topics outside their field of study | Avoids topics outside their comfort zone |
| Seeks out views that differ from their own in order to understand them | Dismisses unfamiliar views without engaging with them |

### Out of scope

- General knowledge: knowing a lot is not the same as wanting to know more.
- Intelligence and academic performance.
- Thrill-seeking or risk-taking.
- *Boundary with Critical Thinking:* CU3 concerns the **willingness** to engage with challenging views. Whether a person **evaluates** them well belongs to Critical Thinking (CT2, CT3).

### Planned measures

| Code | Measure | Type |
|---|---|---|
| CU-SR | Self-report, balanced keying; IPIP intellect or openness items where they align with a facet (public domain). Other published scales, such as the Five-Dimensional Curiosity Scale (Kashdan et al., 2018), only after a licence check | Self-report |
| CU-T1 | Information seeking with costs: rate prior knowledge and curiosity per question, then choose which answers to obtain with a limited budget or by waiting; scored relative to the stated knowledge gap. Topics span many fields, mainly business, psychology, economics and politics | Behaviour |
| CU-T2 | Question generation: write questions about a stimulus; classified as explanatory (why, how) or factual (what, when) by a transparent rule set plus manual review | Production |

### Coverage

| Facet | CU-SR | CU-T1 Information seeking | CU-T2 Question generation |
|---|---|---|---|
| CU1 Information seeking | ✓ | ✓ primary | |
| CU2 Questioning | ✓ | | ✓ primary |
| CU3 Openness to the unfamiliar | ✓ | | |

**Gap.** CU3 is measured only by self-report. CU-T2 asks students to write in English, so it carries some language load (see Communication, out of scope).

### Decisions and open questions

**Decided by the owner (26 November 2026):**

- CU3 (openness) is kept, measured by self-report only for now.
- CU-T1 topics span many fields, mainly business, psychology, economics and politics. Political topics must be factual and non-partisan, and must not depend on one country's institutions (`docs/REVIEW-CHECKLIST.md`, section 3). Every answer cites a source, and contested claims are described as contested (audit A-3).

No open questions remain for this skill.

## 3. Critical Thinking

**Working definition.** Evaluating claims, evidence and reasoning, including one's own, in order to reach well-justified conclusions held with appropriate confidence.

### CT1. Logical reasoning

Drawing valid conclusions from given information, and recognising which cases would and would not test a claim. The conditional-reasoning measure belongs to the selection-task family (Wason, 1968); abstract and rule-based versions differ strongly in difficulty (Griggs & Cox, 1982; Cosmides, 1989), so they are reported separately.

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| Identifies the cases that could prove a rule wrong, not only the cases that fit it | Looks only for examples that confirm a rule |
| Distinguishes what follows necessarily from what merely seems likely | Treats plausible conclusions as if they were proven |

### CT2. Argument evaluation and perspective-taking

Identifying assumptions, flaws and the strength of evidence in an argument, including arguments for positions one does not hold; stating another view fairly before judging it.

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| Spots an unstated assumption, or a gap between the evidence and the conclusion | Accepts a conclusion because it sounds reasonable or matches their own view |
| Judges which evidence would most strengthen or weaken a claim | Treats all evidence as equally strong, or relies on a single anecdote |
| States an opposing view in a form its supporters would accept, then evaluates it | Argues against a weak or distorted version of the opposing view |
| Applies the same standard of evidence to arguments they agree with and arguments they disagree with | Is critical only of arguments whose conclusion they dislike |

*Boundary with Curiosity:* CU3 concerns the **willingness** to engage with other views. CT2 concerns how **accurately** a person represents them and how **well** they evaluate them.

### CT3. Calibration and self-correction

Holding conclusions with a confidence that matches the evidence, and revising them when new evidence arrives.

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| Is more confident when right and less confident when wrong | Is equally confident whether right or wrong, usually too confident |
| Changes a view when shown better evidence, and can say why | Defends a view whatever the new evidence |

### CT4. Statistical and base-rate reasoning

Reasoning correctly about proportions, samples, chance and how common something is, when judging a claim.

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| Asks how common something is in general before judging a single case | Judges a single case only by how well it matches a description, ignoring how common it is |
| Treats a result from a small or self-selected sample with caution | Generalises from a few cases or a striking example |

### Out of scope

- Subject knowledge in any particular discipline.
- Mathematical skill beyond basic proportions.
- Scepticism as a personality trait: doubting everything is not the same as evaluating well.
- Ethical reasoning (deciding what is right). Perspective-taking is in scope as part of CT2.
- Statistical skill beyond proportions, percentages and simple chance: no formulas, and no calculation that needs a calculator.

Statistical and base-rate reasoning is a standard part of published critical-thinking batteries (Stanovich, West & Toplak, 2016). No proprietary test items (for example, Watson-Glaser or HCTA) are used.

### Planned measures

| Code | Measure | Type |
|---|---|---|
| CT-SR | Self-report, balanced keying per facet | Self-report |
| CT-T1 | Conditional reasoning: abstract and rule-based items reported separately; varied negations so the answer pattern differs; all-or-nothing scoring per item | Performance |
| CT-T2 | Argument evaluation and perspective-taking: identify the flaw, the assumption or the strongest evidence in short business or news scenarios; some items ask for the fairest statement of a view, or the strongest objection to a position | Performance |
| CT-T3 | Calibration with a revision step: two-choice factual or estimation items with confidence ratings; on some items the student then sees new evidence and may revise the answer and confidence. Scored for accuracy, over- or under-confidence, and appropriate revision | Performance |
| CT-T4 | Statistical and base-rate reasoning: short scenarios in which the answer depends on a base rate, a sample size or a proportion; one defensible key per item | Performance |

### Coverage

| Facet | CT-SR | CT-T1 Conditional reasoning | CT-T2 Argument evaluation | CT-T3 Calibration and revision | CT-T4 Statistical reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CT1 Logical reasoning | ✓ | ✓ primary | | | |
| CT2 Argument evaluation and perspective-taking | ✓ | | ✓ primary | | |
| CT3 Calibration and self-correction | ✓ | | | ✓ primary | |
| CT4 Statistical and base-rate reasoning | ✓ | | ✓ (some evidence items) | | ✓ primary |

**Time.** Four tasks plus a revision step will exceed the audit's target of 8 to 10 minutes per skill (audit section 5). The owner accepted this; pilot timings will decide whether tasks are shortened. CT4 items use plain numbers and short text to keep the reading and calculation load low for students writing in a second language.

### Decisions and open questions

**Decided by the owner (26 November 2026):**

- Perspective-taking is in scope and ethical reasoning is not. To keep to four facets, perspective-taking is part of CT2 rather than a facet of its own.
- Statistical and base-rate reasoning is a fourth facet (CT4), with its own task (CT-T4).
- CT-T3 gains a revision step, so self-correction is measured directly.

No open questions remain for this skill. The build plan's S7 prompt describes three tasks; it must be read together with this section (see the decisions log in `audit/BUILD-PLAN.md`).

## 4. Creativity

**Working definition.** Generating ideas that are both new and useful for the purpose, and finding problems worth solving.

The "new and useful" definition follows the standard definition in creativity research (Runco & Jaeger, 2012). Facets CR1 to CR3 follow the divergent-thinking tradition associated with Guilford (1967); the categories used to score flexibility will be written for this tool and are not taken from Guilford.

### CR1. Fluency

Producing many relevant ideas in response to an open problem.

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| Keeps producing relevant ideas after the obvious ones are used up | Stops after the first few obvious ideas |
| Produces ideas steadily throughout the time available | Produces most ideas in the first minute, then stalls |

### CR2. Flexibility

Producing ideas of different kinds, from different categories or approaches.

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| Moves between very different kinds of idea, for example from using an object as a tool to using it as a material | Produces many variations of the same idea |
| Deliberately looks at the problem from another angle when ideas run out | Stays with one way of seeing the problem |

### CR3. Originality

Producing ideas that few others produce, while remaining workable.

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| Offers uncommon ideas that still clearly work for the purpose | Offers mainly the ideas most people give first |
| Combines ideas from unrelated areas | Stays within the usual context of the problem |

### CR4. Problem finding

Spotting and framing problems, needs or opportunities that have not been stated.

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| Identifies problems in a situation that others overlook, including indirect consequences | Identifies only the problems that are stated or obvious |
| Restates a vague situation as a specific problem that could be worked on | Leaves the problem as vague as it was given |

### Out of scope

- Artistic or aesthetic skill.
- Creative achievement: the tool measures creative *thinking*, not a record of creative output.
- Selecting and refining the best idea (convergent judgement), a possible later addition.
- Unusualness without usefulness: a bizarre idea that would not work does not count as original.

Divergent-thinking tasks measure part of creative *potential*, and results must be described that way.

### Planned measures

| Code | Measure | Type |
|---|---|---|
| CR-SR | Self-report, balanced keying | Self-report |
| CR-T1 | Alternate uses with a fixed object per form; scored for fluency, flexibility (revised category list) and originality against a provisional frequency table, to be replaced by pilot norms; unscorable responses detected | Production |
| CR-T2 | Problem finding or consequences ("What would happen if…"), scored against a rubric | Production |

### Coverage

| Facet | CR-SR | CR-T1 Alternate uses | CR-T2 Problem finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| CR1 Fluency | ✓ | ✓ primary | ✓ (count of distinct problems) |
| CR2 Flexibility | ✓ | ✓ primary | ✓ if the rubric has categories |
| CR3 Originality | ✓ | ✓ primary | ✓ if the rubric rates originality |
| CR4 Problem finding | ✓ | | ✓ primary |

**Gap.** Whether CR-T2 contributes to CR1 to CR3 depends on the rubric, which is not yet written. Both tasks ask students to write in English, so typing speed and English vocabulary affect fluency; see audit E-1.

### Decisions and open questions

**Decided by the owner (26 November 2026):**

- Problem finding (CR4) is kept.
- Originality should be scored automatically where possible. This is bounded by the technical conventions in `CLAUDE.md`:
  - A semantic-distance method may run in the browser only if its data ships with the tool as a static file. No network calls.
  - Language-model scoring stays behind the disabled adapter (audit F-5). It is switched on only after data protection approval, because responses would leave the browser.
  - Until an automated method has been compared with human ratings in the pilot, the frequency-norm score is the reported score, and any automated score is recorded alongside it, not in its place.

No open questions remain for this skill.

## 5. Communication

**Working definition.** Conveying meaning in writing so that the intended reader understands it accurately and with little effort.

### CM1. Clarity and structure

Making the main point clear and easy to find, and making every reference unambiguous.

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| States the main point first, then the supporting detail | Puts the main point after background or politeness |
| Leaves no doubt about who or what each word such as "it" or "they" refers to | Writes sentences that a reader can understand in two ways |

### CM2. Audience and register

Adapting words, tone and level of detail to the reader and the situation.

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| Replaces specialist terms with plain words when the reader is not a specialist | Uses the same vocabulary for every reader |
| Uses a formal tone for a formal reader and a relaxed one where it fits | Mixes formal and casual tone in the same message |

### CM3. Explicit reasoning

Giving the reasons and links a reader needs to follow the path from facts to a conclusion.

| Strong | Developing |
|---|---|
| States why a decision follows from the facts given | Presents a conclusion with no reason, leaving the reader to guess |
| Uses linking words ("because", "so", "however") that match the actual logic | Lists facts without showing how they connect |

### Out of scope

- Spoken presentation, listening and non-verbal communication (possible later additions).
- Grammar and spelling accuracy as such.
- General English proficiency.

Any written task in English partly reflects English proficiency. The tool limits this by favouring selection tasks, never scoring spelling or grammar, and checking items for bias against students writing in a second language during the pilot (audit section 6, point 4).

### Planned measures

| Code | Measure | Type |
|---|---|---|
| CM-SR | Self-report, balanced keying | Self-report |
| CM-T1 | "Which is clearer?" paired comparisons; selection only, fixed key | Receptive |
| CM-T2 | Diagnose: identify the problem in an *unmarked* passage (ambiguity, buried main point, jargon, missing reasoning, wrong register) | Receptive |
| CM-T3 | One rewrite, scored by a transparent checklist that the student also uses to check their own work; coach or language-model scoring optional later | Productive |
| CM-X1 | Optional, not in the first version's score: self-report items and scenario-based judgement items about spoken situations (for example, how to open a presentation, or how to respond when listeners look confused). Reported separately from the written score | Self-report and judgement |

Passages in CM-T1 to CM-T3 use both business writing (emails, briefs, reports) and academic writing (essay paragraphs, summaries of findings), in roughly equal numbers on each form.

The receptive tasks (CM-T1, CM-T2) carry most of the score, to limit the advantage of native English speakers.

### Coverage

| Facet | CM-SR | CM-T1 Which is clearer? | CM-T2 Diagnose | CM-T3 Rewrite |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CM1 Clarity and structure | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| CM2 Audience and register | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| CM3 Explicit reasoning | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |

Each facet needs items of its own in every task, so that facet scores can be reported separately. The item bank (`docs/ITEM-BANK.md`) records the facet of every item.

### Decisions and open questions

**Decided by the owner (26 November 2026):**

- The first version measures written communication. Spoken communication may be added as self-report and scenario-based judgement items (CM-X1), reported separately. These items cannot observe actual speaking, so they do not change the working definition, and results must not present them as a measure of speaking skill.
- Passages use both business and academic writing.

No open questions remain for this skill.

**Decided by the owner (26 November 2026):**
